Division 03

Polygraph Services

A polygraph examination is a structured credibility assessment, conducted with the examinee's written informed consent and set out in a written report. It is designed to support an investigation or an internal process: one input alongside the other evidence, never a replacement for it.

PSIRA Registered Confidential National Coverage
The division

An examination that informs a decision.

A polygraph examination records physiological responses (respiration, cardiovascular activity and electrodermal activity) while a trained examiner puts a short, previously reviewed set of questions to the examinee. What it produces is a structured assessment of credibility on those specific questions, documented in a written report. It is not a truth machine and it does not read thoughts. It gives you an examiner's reasoned opinion on defined issues, which you then weigh against everything else you know.

In South Africa a polygraph examination sits inside employment and labour law rather than above it, and we run ours accordingly. We do not conduct an examination without the examinee's written informed consent, obtained after the purpose, the procedure and the questions have been explained, and an examinee remains free to decline or to stop at any point. A result on its own does not establish that misconduct occurred and should never be treated as a ground for dismissal. How an outcome is used in a disciplinary or contractual process is a decision for the employer and its legal advisers, taken on the whole body of evidence.

Used properly, the examination is an investigative instrument: it narrows a field, tests a specific account against a known event, or gives a screening decision or an internal enquiry a consistent and documented input. The capability can stand on its own, or form part of a broader investigation, a vetting process or an employer's own internal enquiry. Because INTEGRI runs investigation capability alongside this division, an examination rarely stands alone: the structured pre-test interview is informed by what investigators have already established, and anything the examination surfaces can be followed up with further enquiry or a properly recorded statement. Every file is handled in confidence, and every examination ends in a written report, so that a reader who was not in the room can see exactly what was asked, what was recorded and what the examiner concluded.

What this covers

Examinations supporting investigations

A polygraph examination used as one input within an active investigation: a narrow set of previously reviewed questions about a defined event, weighed against the rest of the evidence rather than ahead of it.

Screening examinations

Consent-based examinations supporting a screening or vetting process, structured around declared history and the specific requirements attached to the position or appointment.

Internal enquiry support

Examinations forming part of an employer's own internal enquiry, written up so that the people running that enquiry can read the examiner's opinion and weigh it alongside everything else in front of them.

Our method

How we approach it.

The sequence is fixed, it is explained to the examinee before it begins, and it is recorded in the report afterwards.

01

Pre-test interview

The examination begins with a conversation, not an instrument. Once the examinee has agreed to attend, the examiner explains the purpose and the procedure, establishes any medical or personal factor that would make testing inappropriate, and reviews every question that will be asked. Nothing is put during the test that was not covered first.

02

Written informed consent

Participation is voluntary. Before any data is collected the examinee signs a consent document recording that the procedure has been explained, that they understand who will receive the report, and that they may withdraw at any point. Without that signed consent, the examination does not proceed.

03

In-test data collection

Sensors record respiration, cardiovascular activity and electrodermal activity while the reviewed questions are put in a controlled sequence, normally repeated across several charts. Conditions, wording and sequence are held consistent so that the recorded data is comparable rather than anecdotal.

04

Analysis and written report

The examiner analyses the recorded charts and writes the examination up: the questions put, the data obtained, the method of analysis and a stated conclusion on each issue, together with the limits of that conclusion. The report is released to the commissioning client on the terms set out in the consent document.

Before you commission one

Common questions.

What does a polygraph examination actually produce?
A written report containing the examiner's opinion on a defined set of questions, based on physiological data recorded during the examination. It is a structured credibility assessment rather than proof of anything: it tells you how an examinee responded to specific, previously reviewed questions, which is useful information to weigh alongside documents, statements, system records and the rest of your evidence. We will not tell you that a result proves misconduct, because it does not.
Is participation voluntary, and what happens if someone declines?
Participation is voluntary. We do not examine anyone who has not given written informed consent after the procedure has been explained to them, and an examinee may decline beforehand or withdraw during the examination, at which point the examiner stops. How a refusal is treated inside an employment relationship is a matter for the employer and its own legal advisers; it is not something we advise on and not something we record as an adverse finding.
Who receives the results, and how is the file handled?
The written report goes to the person or organisation that commissioned the examination, on the terms set out in the consent document the examinee signs before testing, so the examinee knows in advance who will read it. Charts, interview notes and reports are held in confidence and are not discussed outside that agreed circle. Where our investigation or forensic divisions are working on the same matter, the file stays under the same confidentiality terms.
How should a result be used in a disciplinary process?
As one input alongside the rest of the evidence, and never as a substitute for a properly run process. A polygraph result on its own does not establish that misconduct occurred and should not be treated as a ground for dismissal. In practice the useful outcome is direction: an issue that warrants further enquiry, an account that can now be tested against documents, or a matter that can reasonably be closed. Employers should take their own legal advice on how any result is handled in a hearing.
